Enzymology

Manifestation of life, in molecular terms, are consequences of interaction of the chemical components of the living system. At the heart of these interactions are the degradation of nutrients to generate metabolic energy and precursors, to synthesise its components that support its structural organisation, and perform all forms of biological work by making use of the precursor and energy obtained through biodegradation. These interactions are kinetically controlled, which suppresses any tendency of the process toward attainment of the state of thermodynamical equilibrium. In living systems thermodynamic equilibrium state leads to death.

Temperature and concentration of the reacting substances are two of the factors which affect the kinetics or rate of a chemical reaction [interaction]. Higher temperature and concentration lead to greater rate of chemical reaction. But in living systems, temperature and concentration of metabolites are moderately low; these lead to very slow chemical reactions. Thus, biochemical processes in living systems proceed under very mild physico-chemical conditions. On the contrary, however, biochemical reactions in living systems rather proceed at very great speed. This is so because of the presence of a very potent catalysts called enzymes.

Enzymes were early discovered in food digestion in saliva and stomach through conversion of starch to sugar. Louis Pasteur concluded that fermentation of sugar into alcohol by yeast is catalysed by "ferment" which was inseparable from the yeast cells. Frederick W. Kuhne called this ferment molecule enzyme, meaning en → in and zyme → fermenting. Later it became established that enzymes are as capable of working inside a cell as outside of it, to accelerate [speed] an already going or thermodynamically feasible reaction. At the end of the reaction, enzymes are as potent as they were at the beginning of the reaction.

All enzymes are protein except catalytic RNA molecules. As protein enzyme catalytic activities depends on the integrity of their native protein conformation, if an enzyme is denatured or dissociated into its subunit, catalytic activity is usually lost. If an enzyme is broken down into its component amino acids, its catalytic activity is always destroyed. Thus the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structures of protein enzymes are essential to their catalytic activity.

Classification of enzymes

Earlier in molecular biology, enzyme did not have any system of nomenclature or classification until 20th century when enzymes were known with trivial unscientific names emanated from tradition without saying anything about the substrate, e.g. ptyalin, pepsin, trypsin, etc. Later the trivial nomenclature was replaced with a rational and more scientific system by adding the suffix "ase" to root words denoting the substrate, e.g. arginase which acts on arginine etc. In 1961 the International Union of Biochemistry [I.U.B] brought a system of nomenclature and classification by considering the substrate and the type of the reaction being catalysed. Under the I.U.B system, enzymes are divided into six (6) classes on the basis of the reactions they catalyse.

Class 1: Oxidoreductases

Enzymes that catalyse oxidation-reduction reaction of metabolism. Oxidation is removal of proton [electron] from the substrate while reduction is addition of proton (electron) to the acceptor molecule.

SH2 + A ⇌ S + AH2 (oxidoreductase; donor : acceptor oxidoreductase)

SH2 is the reduced substrate, A is the oxidised acceptor, S is the oxidised substrate, AH2 is the reduced acceptor.

Lactate + NAD+ ⇌ Pyruvate + NADH(H+) CH3–CHOH–COOH → CH3–CO–COOH, lactate dehydrogenase

Other examples of oxidoreductase are catalase, peroxidase, electron transport protease, anaerobic dehydrogenase.

Class 2: Transferases

Are enzymes that catalyse the reaction of inter-molecular transfer of atom or atomic groups (donor–acceptor transferase).

S–G + A–0 ⇌ S–0 + A–G
Glucose + ATP → Glucose-6-phosphate + ADP hexokinase (D-hexose-6-phosphotransferase)

The hexokinase is known in I.U.B as D-hexose-6-phosphotransferase. Other examples of transferases are: (1) Aminotransferase (2) Amidotransferase (3) Acyltransferase.

Class 3: Hydrolases

Enzymes that catalyse hydrolysis reaction by cleavage of bonds in organic compound with influence of water.

R1–R2 + H2O → R1–H + R2–OH
L-Arginine + H2O → Urea + L-Ornithine arginase · I.U.B: substrate-hydrolase

The I.U.B of arginase is L-arginine-ureohydrolase. Other examples: (1) Esterases (2) Glycosidases (3) Peptidases (4) Amidase (5) Polyphosphatase.

Class 4: Lyases

Enzymes that catalyse non-hydrolytic removal of group from substrate to form double bonds or attachment of groups at double bond positions in the substrate. The reaction usually accompanied by the release of products like CO2, H2O, NH3 etc. The I.U.B for lyase is in form of substrate–the group removed (or attached) lyase.

L-malate ⇌ Fumarate + H2O fumarase · HO–CH(COOH)–CH2–COOH ⇌ HOOC–CH=CH–COOH

Fumarase or fumarate hydratase is known as L-malate-hydro-lyase. E.g. carbon–carbon lyase, carbon–nitrogen lyase, carbon–oxygen lyase.

Class 5: Isomerases

Enzymes that catalyse geometrical or structural change within their substrate molecules. These can be intramolecular transfer of atoms or atomic groups, changes in the spatial disposition of atoms, intramolecular displacement of double bonds. The class include cis-trans isomerase, keto-enol isomerase, mutases, racemase, epimerase etc. I.U.B system is substrate–isomerase or substrate-type of reaction isomerase.

L-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate ⇌ Dihydroxyacetone phosphate triosephosphate isomerase

The I.U.B of triosephosphate isomerase is D-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate ketol isomerase.

Class 6: Ligases [Synthetases]

Enzyme that catalyse joining together of two molecules [condensation] with cleavage (hydrolysis) of ATP. The I.U.B name of ligase is "X : Y ligase [ADP]".

X + Y + ATP → X–Y + ADP + Pi
L-Glutamate + NH3 + ATP → Glutamine + ADP + Pi glutamine synthetase

Mechanism of action of enzymes

Structure of enzyme

Structure-functionally, enzymes can be classified into two broad groups namely: one-component protein enzyme and two-component conjugated protein enzyme. While the one-component enzymes are composed only polypeptide chain, the two-component enzyme contain polypeptide and non-protein structure. Enzyme together with the non-protein is called holoenzyme; the protein part of holoenzyme is called apoenzyme while the non-protein component is called cofactor. This could be metals or an organic chemical group.

Some metals in enzyme structure may participate directly in the catalytic process or may aid in maintaining the native conformation of enzyme. The metals which stabilise the native conformation of enzymes are called activator. The cofactor which tightly bound in the enzyme structure that can not easily separated is called prosthetic group, while cofactor that are loosely bound is called coenzyme. They may also called co-substrates because they participate stoichiometrically in reaction and consumed along with the main substrates. The role of coenzyme in conjugated enzyme is usually played by vitamin or its derivatives. This apoenzyme cannot carry-out catalytic activities in isolation only when it form holoenzyme with cofactors.

Note: (1) The apoenzyme amplifies the catalytic ability in holoenzyme while the cofactor stabilises the apoenzyme [protein] against denaturation. (2) Cofactor has intrinsic function which form catalytic centre while the apoenzyme makes this function real by a region in its structure where the cofactor specifically bind. This region is called co-factor binding domain.

Catalytic centre, substrate centre and active centre

Enzymes contain non-protein components that could interact with the substrate to bring biochemical transformation; this intrinsic function is performed by a region of protein enzyme molecule. This centre is called catalytic centre of the enzyme molecule. This centre contains combinations of amino acid residues. Examples: serine, histidine, arginine, cysteine, aspartic and glutamic acid, threonine and tyrosine. These amino acid radicals play the same role with coenzyme in conjugated enzymes.

(4) Amino acid residues converging from different points of the polypeptide chain is folded into the native tertiary structure to form catalytic centre. Thus any changes in tertiary structure caused by certain agents would lead to deformation of the catalytic centre and catalytic activities of the enzyme.

(5) Apart from catalytic centre formed by cofactor or assembly of specific amino acid residues, enzymes possess two other sites in its centre: the substrate centre and allosteric centre.

Substrate centre is the site in enzyme molecule responsible for the anchorage of the compound undergoing biochemical transformation; the fixation of this substrate on this site involves ε-amino group of lysine, carboxylic group of glutamic acid and sulfhydryl group of cystein where the hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interaction is absolutely involved to form enzyme-substrate interaction. For a specific activity of holoenzyme, active centre of enzyme is considered rather than substrate centre.

The active centre is the part of enzyme molecule which binds to the substrate and cofactor to bring complexity well adapted for biochemical transformation. It exist as cleft.

Molecular forms of enzymes

Enzyme exist in several forms within a cell, organism. Difference modification (form) of enzyme that catalyse the same biochemical reaction are called multiple molecular form of the same enzyme. There two process that brings modification of enzyme to different forms:

  • Biochemical origin which consist the post-translation modification of the nascent enzyme protein [new enzyme protein]. This can be by proteolytic cleavage, methylation, phosphorylation, glycosylation, acetylation etc. These chemical modifications brings about changes in catalytic activities of the enzyme, e.g. phosphorylase.
  • Genetic origin: the genetically determined molecular form of enzyme is called isoenzymes [isozymes]. They are different versions of the same enzyme that catalyse the same biochemical reaction with the same coenzyme but have different structure, properties and genetic gene. They differ in electrophoretic mobility, immunological reaction and tissue and organ location.

Example 1: Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Of glycolytic sequence [convert pyruvate to lactate] it exist in different isoenzymes forms. It is tetrameric molecule consisting of four subunits of two types H and M.

LDH1LDH2LDH3LDH4LDH5
HHHHHHHMHHMMHMMMMMMM
Electrophoretic spectrum of lactate dehydrogenase isoenzymes of the serum: anode (+) ← origin (at LDH3) → cathode (−); bands LDH1 and LDH2 migrate toward the anode, LDH4 and LDH5 toward the cathode.

Note: LDH1 predominates in heart [H stands for heart]. LDH5 predominates in muscle [M stands for muscle].

During electrophoretic separation, LDH1 is the fast migrating running closest to the anode while LDH5 is slowest running closest to the cathode. Tissue damage occurring in some pathological states results in increase in some isoenzyme fractions in the serum. Examples: (1) myocardial infarction [increase in LDH1] (2) muscular dystrophy [increase in LDH4 and LDH5] (3) leukaemia [increase in LDH3 and LDH4]. All these pathological states can be diagnosed by electrophoretic analysis of LDH isoenzyme spectrum. Note the increase in these isoenzyme in serum means that the tissue where the one predominately occurs had damage.

Example, leukaemia [cancer of white blood cell]: come from bone marrow and lymphoid tissues; these tissue normally have high LDH3. When leukaemia cells (1) proliferate fast → cell turnover lost, and LDH leaks into blood; (2) live in low O2 bone marrow → more LDH3 enzyme expressed; (3) undergo lysis from chemo or tumor burden → release intracellular LDH3.

Example 2: Creatine kinase (CK)

It catalyse conversion of creatine phosphate to creatine and release energy in form of ATP. The enzyme is isoenzyme that exist in chimeric with subunits of two types B and M, to generate three isoenzyme: CK1 [in brain], CK2 [myocardium] and CK3 [in skeletal muscle]. B stands for brain, M stands for muscle and myocardium. Pathological condition of these organs and tissue result in abnormal increase in the corresponding isoenzyme fraction in serum [blood].

Mechanism [steps] of action of enzymes

Enzyme, whether one-component or two-component, have similar mechanism of action due to functional similarity of active centre of all enzymes; the formation of enzyme-substrate complex is the central mechanism of action of all enzymes. The mechanism was divided into four steps [Brown 1902] and [Michaelis and Menten 1913]. These steps are:

S + E ⇌I ES ⇌II ES′ ⇌III EP ⇌IV E + P
  • Step I: Enzyme-substrate complex is formed stabilised by chemical and physico-chemical forces such as covalent, ionic, hydrogen bond, hydrophobic and van der Waal's interaction.
  • Step II: Enzyme-substrate complex undergoes activation change which prepare it for biochemical reaction.
  • Step III: is the stage of biochemical transformation of enzyme-substrate complex to enzyme-product complex.
  • Step IV: liberation of product from enzyme-product complex with regeneration of free enzyme E.

Note: the mechanism is cooperatively, specifically and definite order in space and time unlike chemical catalyst.

Example: acetylcholine (mediator in transmission of nerve impulse) catalysed by acetylcholine esterase

Step I: the enzyme is one-component protein with four amino acid residue [glutamic acid, serine, histidine and tyrosine] that form the active centre. Enzyme-substrate complex form by electrostatic interaction between the positive (+) charge (nitrogen) in acetylcholine and negative (−) charge in carboxylic group of active centre glutamate.

Step II: formation of hydrogen bond between OH group of serine and polarised C=O in substrate. Another hydrogen bond is between OH of tyrosine and the ester oxygen.

Step III: the formation of these hydrogen bonds result to weakening of ester linkage. The imidazole histidine group of the active centre attracts proton from OH of serine, thereby causes breaking down of ester bond of the substrate with proton crossing from tyrosine OH to choline group.

Step IV: hydrolysis of the active centre, result to liberation of the reaction product [choline] and regenerate OH group of the amino acid radicals; then the enzyme is ready for the next act of catalyst. (Acetic acid, CH3COOH, is also released.)

Important features of enzyme catalysis

  • The conformational changes of the enzyme molecule during the process is brought by participation of the whole polypeptide structure of the enzyme.
  • The substrate fit into the enzyme active centre in the manner of "a key to the lock". The key to lock concept was formulated by Fischer 1894 state that "Specific action of an enzyme is predetermined by strict correspondence of the geometrical structure of the substrate to the enzyme active centre".
  • Koshland 20th century modification of key-to-lock by Fischer states that the active centre of enzyme is conformational flexible; when substrate approaches the active site, the change interaction induce complementary conformational change in both substrate and active site of enzyme to bring a corresponding fit.

(1) Thermolabileness

Is the duality and opposite influence of temperature on enzyme action. It enhances the rate of formation of enzyme-substrate complex at certain temperature range [10°C – 50°C] and causes denaturation and inactivation at extreme value. This cause by gradually accumulation of denatured, inactive enzyme in the medium. (Graph: rate [Mm/min] against T (°C), peaking at the optimum temperature near 40–50°C then falling.)

(2) pH effect

The effect of pH or concentration of hydrogen ion on enzyme activity is based mainly on its effect on the active centre. This is determined by the degree of ionization of groups in the active centre. The number of anion and cation formed during ionization of the group in active centre affect the formation of enzyme-substrate complex; it also affect the ionization of substrate to form enzyme-product complex. Enzyme has a pH optimum at which it displays maximum activity [maximal accumulation and interaction of the most charge state of enzyme and substrate]. As the pH moves away from optimum, denature occurred and there is decrease in reaction rate.

(3) Specificity of enzyme action

This is a discriminatory action of an enzyme on a substrate, due to complementarity between the configuration of active centre and that of substrate. Three types of enzyme specificity:

  • Absolute specificity: is observed when one particular enzyme catalyse a particular reaction or acts on a particular compound. E.g. glucokinase catalyse phosphorylation of glucose only and not phosphorylation of another hexose. Urease catalyse only the hydrolysis of urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
  • Group specificity: is observed when an enzyme catalyse a particular reaction irrespective of the substrate; what matters is the type of chemical bond formed or broken in the substrate. E.g. hexokinase catalyse ATP-mediated phosphorylation of hexoses, whether glucose, galactose etc.
  • Stereochemical specificity: enzyme catalysing specific transformation of a particular isomers of a compound and not mediating the transformation of the corresponding antipode. E.g. amino acid transaminase catalyse the transamination of only L-amino acids.

(4) Enzyme dependence on activators activity

Substances called activator such as coenzymes, cofactors enhance the formation of enzyme-substrate complex by stabilising the tertiary or quaternary structure of allosteric enzyme when bind at allosteric site. This bring conformational change that facilitate catalysis.

(5) Enzyme dependence on inhibitors activity

Some factors like heat, concentrated acids, salts, and heavy metals decrease enzyme reaction non-specifically; they have no information about the mechanism of action, while some factors called inhibitor are chemical agents which specifically decrease the rate of biochemical reaction. It could be reversible and irreversible inhibition.

Enzyme inhibition

Irreversible inhibition

Interaction between enzyme and inhibitor to form enzyme-inhibitor complex is irreversible.

E + I → EI

This complex doesn't yield product and is stabilised by covalent bonds. There are two main mode of action of irreversible inhibitor: (a) First mode: when the inhibitor has structural similarity with the substrate, it replace the substrate by chemical modification and blocking the active centre which permanently switches the enzyme out of the action. (b) Second mode: the inhibitor has no structural resemblance with the substrate; rather the inhibitor acts by chemically modifying the enzyme outside the active centre resulting to irreversible change in conformation of the active centre and that of enzyme molecule.

Reversible inhibition

This occur when the interaction of the inhibitor and enzyme is reversible.

E + I ⇌ EI

There are three types of reversible inhibition: (a) competitive (b) non-competitive (c) uncompetitive.

(a) Competitive inhibition

This occur when the inhibitor binds only to the free enzyme, denying substrate access to the active site. It has increase in Km. It is very serious at low substrate concentration. Competitive inhibition can be reduced by increase in substrate concentration, e.g. malonic acid on succinate dehydrogenase.

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P    E + I ⇌ EI

Michaelis plot and Lineweaver-Burk plot: Vmax unchanged, Km increases (the lines meet on the 1/V axis, with a steeper slope with increasing inhibitor).

(b) Uncompetitive inhibition

This occur when inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex and not to free enzyme. It decrease the Vmax, ES concentration and Km; high substrate cannot alleviate it, rather it becomes worse. Examples: (1) liver-alcohol dehydrogenase (2) acetylcholinesterase.

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P    ES + I ⇌ ESI

Lineweaver-Burk plot: parallel lines, the inhibited line shifted up (intercepts −1/Km and −1/Vmax both move).

(c) Non-competitive inhibition

The inhibitor displays random binding either to the free enzyme or to the enzyme-substrate complex. Vmax decrease without change in Km.

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P    E + I ⇌ EI    ES + I ⇌ ESI

Lineweaver-Burk plot: lines meet on the x-axis at −1/Km; the inhibited line is steeper.

Enzyme kinetics

A biochemical kinetics is the study of the dependence of the rates of enzyme-catalysed reaction on the nature of biochemical reactant and on the conditions of their interactions. Reactant means substrate and enzyme. Since the reaction is specific it depends on temperature, concentration, pH of the medium, presence of activator and inhibitors etc. Basically all biochemical reaction follows the same pattern, that is it has substrate [S], enzyme [E], enzyme-substrate complex [ES] and product [P]; all the reactions are reversible. The slowest and rate-limiting step is the decomposition of the enzyme-substrate complex to give product and the free enzyme.

Michaelis–Menten equation for biochemical kinetics

E + S ⇌k1, V1k−1, V−1 ES ⇌k2, V2k−2, V−2 P + E   (a)

V2 = rate-limiting step of catalysis. k1, k−1, k2 and k−2 are rate constant whereas V1, V−1, V2 and V−2 are the reaction velocities in the directions specified by the arrow. According to law of mass action [rate of reaction is directly proportional to the product of the concentration of reactant]:

V2 = k2[ES]   (b) rate-limiting step

When the rate V2 reaches its maximum then V2 = Vmax and all the enzyme(s) present bound to the substrate. Then:

Vmax = k2[E0]   (c)   [E0] = total concentration of enzyme

Then rate of formation ES complex V1 is proportional to the product of S and E:

V1 = k1[S][E]   (d)

Note: during the reaction the conversion of P to ES is almost not possible and the reversible reaction is ignored. The concentration of ES complex [ES] depends on its formation and the rate of breaking down ES complex into S and E:

V1 = k1[S][E]   (d)
V−1 = k−1[ES]   (e) breaking ES to S

At the steady state when the concentration of ES complex is constant, the rate of its formation (V1) will be equal to the total rate of its decomposition in both directions (V−1 + V2):

V1 = V−1 + V2   (f)
k1[S][E] = k−1[ES] + k2[ES]   (g)

At equilibrium, concentration of enzyme remaining [E] is equal to concentration of total enzyme [E0] minus enzyme in enzyme-substrate complex [ES], i.e.

[E0] − [ES] = [E]

From eqn (g):

k1[S][E] = k−1[ES] + k2[ES]
k1([E0] − [ES])[S] = k−1[ES] + k2[ES]
k1([E0] − [ES])[S] = (k−1 + k2)[ES]   (j)
[E0][S] − [ES][S] = (k−1 + k2)/k1 [ES]   (k)

Let (k−1 + k2)/k1 be expressed as one constant Km called Michaelis constant. Then:

[E0][S] − [ES][S] = Km[ES]
[E0][S] = Km[ES] + [ES][S]
[E0][S] = [ES](Km + [S])
[ES] = [E0][S] / (Km + [S])

From eqn (b), V2 = k2[ES], so [ES] = V2/k2, and Vmax = k2[E0] from eqn (c):

[E0][S] / (Km + [S]) = V2 / k2
[E0][S]k2 = V2(Km + [S])
Vmax[S] = V2(Km + [S])
V2 = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])   Michaelis–Menten equation

Michaelis–Menten principle: In 1913 Michaelis and Menten studied the basis of kinetic of enzyme catalysed reaction and formulated a theory which says that an enzyme first of all forms a complex with the substrate which then decomposes to give the product and the free enzyme, i.e.

S + E ⇌ ES ⇌ P + E   (a)

From this analysis they derived equation called Michaelis–Menten equation, i.e. V = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]), where V = rate of decomposition of ES to product; Vmax = rate when all the enzyme has bound to the substrate, called maximum rate; [S] = concentration of the substrate; Km = Michaelis constant. Since the enzyme concentration remains constant, V varies with the concentration of substrate [S]; a graph of the relation is plotted (a hyperbola rising to Vmax; V = Vmax/2 at [S] = Km).

When the maximum rate of reaction is half, the concentration of substrate [S] is called Michaelis constant Km.

To form a linear equation from Michaelis–Menten equation in order to determine Vmax, V, [S] and Km, four other scientists involved with different plots: Lineweaver–Burk; Hofstee–Eadie; Hanes; Eisenthal and Cornish-Bowden.

Lineweaver–Burk plot (double reciprocal plot)

Obtained by plotting 1/V versus 1/[S]. From the equation of Michaelis:

V = Vmax / (Km/[S] + 1)  ⇒  1/V = (Km/[S] + 1) / Vmax
1/V = (Km/Vmax) × (1/[S]) + 1/Vmax   compare with y = mx + c

Note: (1) gradient = Km/Vmax (2) intercept on y-axis = 1/Vmax (3) intercept on x-axis = −1/Km.

Hofstee–Eadie plot

They plotted V/[S] versus V to obtain a straight line graph.

V = Vmax / (1 + Km/[S]) → V + KmV/[S] = Vmax → KmV/[S] = Vmax − V
V/[S] = V(−1/Km) + Vmax/Km

(1) gradient = −1/Km (2) intercept on y-axis = Vmax/Km (3) intercept on x-axis = Vmax.

Hanes plot

He plotted [S]/V versus [S] to get a straight line graph, from 1/V = 1/Vmax + (Km/[S])(1/Vmax), multiply all with [S]:

[S]/V = [S]/Vmax + Km/Vmax

(1) gradient = 1/Vmax (2) intercept on y-axis = Km/Vmax (3) intercept on x-axis = −Km.

Regulation, uses and applications of enzymes

The biochemical activities of enzyme and metabolites formed are mostly influence by the activities of the enzyme and the environment surrounding in the cell. This is regulated and coordinated by micro-environmental fine-tunes activities. These regulation can be discussed in following point.

  • Self regulation: this is the regulation of enzyme activity by the concentration of substrate, product and enzyme; when there is accumulation of the product of a distance reaction the rate is influence and this is called negative feedback control.
  • Zymogen activation: zymogens are inactive synthesised extracellular enzymes which are activated and regulated by proteolytic removal of an inhibitory amino acid or peptide moiety from its zymogen structure resulting in the formation of active site. The activation occur in digestive process, regulation of blood clotting and immunological reaction.
  • Hormonal regulation: hormone as a chemical mediator which stimulate or suppress specific metabolic processes in tissues and organs has no direct regulation of enzyme rather causes changes in tissue permeability or transport activity of biological membrane or enhancing rate of formation of mRNA which is translated into the enzyme protein. Apparent regulatory action of hormone on enzyme is like a "remote control agent" which act indirectly through certain mechanism or intermediaries.
  • Covalent modification: enzyme activity can be regulated by introduction or removal of a specific chemical group from the enzyme molecule. This is called modified enzyme which is different from unmodified either by being more or less active. The modifying group include phosphoryl, adenyl, methyl, ADP-ribosyl group. These group are removed from the enzyme by separating the enzyme.
  • Allosteric regulation: allosteric enzyme is an enzyme that contain substrate binding site and allosteric site. If effectors are positive they are called allosteric activator, if they are negative they are called allosteric inhibitor. This regulates enzyme activity by decreasing or increasing enzyme affinity to the substrate [increasing Km and decreasing Km value respectively]. The effect can be the substrate itself, called homotropic allosterism; if it is another compound it is called heterotropic allosterism. The allosteric enzyme is oligomeric protein that contains two or more substrate binding sites; the binding of substrate to the first subunit bring conformational change in neighbouring subunit which alters or modulates the catalytic activities of the enzyme. Note: allosteric enzymes do not obey Michaelis–Menten rate law. The reaction rate doesn't depend hyperbolic on substrate concentration and the Lineweaver–Burk double reciprocal plot are non-linear; their V versus S plots are sigmoidal curve described by Hill's rather than Michaelis equation. This sigmoidal shape is the consequence of cooperativity between the enzyme subunit during catalysis.

Uses of enzymes

Although enzymes are elaborated in living systems, they are capable of carrying out their action outside the cell or outside the organism. This fact is exploited in many fields of practical application:

  • Domestic use: protease that withstand high temperature are ingredient of detergent for cleaning; glucose oxidase and amylase are used for manufacturing wash powder, water oxidase removal of stain.
  • Bakery: amylase and proteinase from fungi are added to the dough to enhance aroma, softness and porosity of the loaves and reduce quantity of sugar.
  • Brewery: amylase catalyse hydrolysis of starch during alcoholic beverage production and use in extraction of juice from fruits during wine and fruit juice production.
  • Medicine: enzyme in medicine consist of two aspect: the first is therapeutic use and second is clinico-diagnostic application of changes in the pattern of enzyme activities of tissue, organs and body fluid.

Therapeutic uses (continued): (1) …use in treating disease of gastro-intestinal tract. (2) Hyaluronidase: enzyme that catalyses depolymerization of hyaluronic acid [ground substance in tissue and joint] and is employed to aid penetration of drug into diseased tissues and joints. (3) Plasmin, urokinase and streptokinase are used to dissolve and eliminate thrombus from blood vessel. (4) Pancreatic deoxyribonuclease is used to treat respiratory tract disease and removal of pus from wound. (5) Asparaginase that catalyses dimination of asparagine [tumor growth factor] is used in treatment of certain kinds of cancer. (6) Cytochrome is used to handle hypoxic complication during heart disease. (7) Lysozyme used in treating conjunctivitis.

Clinical diagnostic applications of enzymes

This can be achieved based on changes of activities of certain enzymes or on changes in isoenzyme spectra of the blood and of the urine. The activities of lactate dehydrogenase, aspartate aminotransferase, creatine kinase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphate aldolase are used in diagnoses of myocardial infarction, muscular dystrophy etc., mostly in disease of the heart using LDH and aspartate aminotransferase. γ-glutamyl transferase is used in immunological rejection of transplant by host; alkaline phosphatase serves for diagnoses of onset of osteomalacia and rickets, and of diseases of the liver.

Ionisation of water, pH and buffers

An important property of water is its ability to dissociate into ions. During dissociation 1 mole of water produce hydronium ion (H3O+) and hydroxyl ion (OH−) or hydrogen (H+) as proton and hydroxyl ion at body temperature; it is very slight.

H2O + H2O → H3O+ + OH−   (1)
H2O → H+ + OH−   (2)

According to Brønsted and Lowry, an acid is a substance which dissociate to yield proton and donate the proton, while base accept proton. I.e. if HA is acid:

HA ⇌ H+ + A−

This process is reversible, meaning none of them can exist without each other. They are referred as conjugate, where A− is the conjugate base and HA is the conjugate acid. To determine the equilibrium constant, ionic product [Kw], pH and pK of ionized biomolecules, the law of mass action is applied. For water:

H2O ⇌ H+ + OH−
KH2O = [H+][OH−] / [H2O]

KH2O is calculated at 25 °C of conductivity of water as 1.8 × 10−16 mol/L. Concentration of water [H2O] = mass of one litre of water / molar mass of water = 1000 g / (16 + 2) = 1000/18 = 55.56 mol/L. Then:

KH2O × [H2O] = [H+][OH−] = Kw (ionic product of water)
Kw = 1.8 × 10−16 × 55.56 = 10−14 mol2/L2

At 25 °C of pure water [OH−] = [H+], then [H+] or [OH−] = √Kw = √10−14 = 10−7 M.

pH and pK

To determine the H+ and OH− of a biomolecule solution, pH and pK are better used because numerical values of [H+] and [OH−] are very small and difficult to applied mathematically. pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration in base 10:

pH = −log10[H+]   or   pH = 1 / log10[H+]

This means that pH is inversely related to [H+]: the smaller the pH value the greater the hydrogen ion concentration and higher the acidity of a solution. This also hold for pK-value. The pH scale is exponential, meaning one-unit increase in the pH value of a solution triggers a 10-time decrease in the hydrogen ion concentration of the solution.

For R–COOH ⇌ R–COO− + H+, according to law of mass action:

K = [R–COO−][H+] / [R–COOH]

At equilibrium [half neutralization], when concentration of conjugate acid and base are equal, [R–COO−] = [R–COOH], so K = [H+]; by taking negative logarithm of both sides, −log K = −log [H+], i.e. pK = pH. Thus pK of acidic solution is the pH-value at which the concentration of conjugate acid species is equal to that of conjugate base species.

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

HA ⇌ H+ + A−; according to law of mass action, K = [H+][A−]/[HA]. Then taking logarithm of both sides:

log K = log [H+] + log([A−]/[HA])
−log K = −log [H+] − log([A−]/[HA])   (multiply through by −1 to get pH)
where −log K = pK and −log [H+] = pH
pK = pH − log([A−]/[HA])
pH = pK + log([A−] / [HA])   Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

Conditions: (1) pH = pK at half neutralization; (2) [A−] = [HA].

Buffering phenomenon

Hydrogen ion plays a special role in biochemical reactions because it is the medium in biofluid and many life processes can only occur at specific concentration of hydrogen ion; deviation from these leads to adverse effect that causes physiological effect in living cells and tissue. Due to adverse effect of deviation from optimal hydrogen ion concentration, the body system needs ability to maintain constant hydrogen ion concentration. This is an intrinsic function of the chemical or biochemical system itself. For a solution to buffer it must contain a mixture of weak acid and its salt. This two component react to keep the pH or hydrogen ion concentration stable at constant value. By buffering we mean the ability of solution to resist change in pH when acid or basic medium is added.

Mechanism of buffering

Buffer contain mixture of weak acid and its salt, i.e. HA (acid) and MA (salt); M = metal, H = hydrogen, A = acid anhydride.

  1. If a strong acid [i.e. H+] is added to the system the incoming H+ reacts with A− from the ionized salt (MA) component of buffer to form HA which is poorly dissociated into H+ / A−; the expected increase in hydrogen ion concentration and decrease in pH in the system does not occur. The system will continue to resist externally induced decrease in pH caused by addition of acid solution as long as the salt MA is continues present in concentration to generate enough A−.
  2. If a strong base [i.e. OH−] is added to the system the coming OH− will react with H+ from ionized weak acid component of the buffer to form water, and because water poorly dissociate into H+ and OH− the expected increase in pH and decrease in hydrogen ion concentration does not occur; the system will continue to prevent such externally induced pH increase as long as HA is present in concentration to supply H+.

Bicarbonate buffer of blood

H+ [from lactic acid produced in muscle tissue during rigorous exercise] is added to blood. As it passes through the tissue, reaction (1) H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 occurs which increase concentration of H2CO3, and in reaction (2) H2CO3 ⇌ H2O + CO2, and increase the concentration of CO2 in blood which increase the pressure of CO2(g) in the air space of lungs; reaction (3) occurs, extra CO2 is exhaled. When the pH of blood plasma is raised [by NH3 produced during protein catabolism], the opposite event occurred: the H+ concentration of blood plasma is lowered, causing more H2CO3 to dissociate into H+ and HCO3−, which in turn causes more CO2(g) from the lungs to dissolve in the blood plasma. The rate of breathing [inhaling & exhaling CO2] can quickly adjust these equilibria to keep the blood pH nearly constant.

H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H2O + CO2(aq) ⇌ CO2(g) Rxn 1 · Rxn 2 · Rxn 3 (aqueous phase: blood in capillaries → gas phase: lung air space)

Protein buffer system

Is the largest buffer system in the body that occur in cells and blood. Protein is made of amino acid; each amino acid has two groups that gain or loss hydrogen ion, i.e. carboxylic group [COOH] and amino group [NH3+], and it can donate and accept H+ [amphoteric].

Case I: acid is added [H+]: protein + H+ → Hprotein; the basic group in protein will accept it and become neutral; pH drops very little. Case II: base is added [OH−]: the acidic groups on the protein donate H+ to neutralize OH−, pH rise very little: Hprotein + OH− → protein + H2O.

Note: only histidine imidazole [pK = 6.0] plays a significant role in the protein buffering system of the blood. Histidine occurs widely in polypeptide chains of haemoglobin and plasma protein; as far as 80% of blood buffering comes from haemoglobin, the plasma proteins contribute 20% buffering.

Solving problems using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

1) Calculate the pKa of lactic acid, given that when the concentration of lactic acid is 0.010 M and the concentration of lactate is 0.087 M, pH is 4.80.

pH = pKa + log([lactate]/[lactic acid])
pKa = pH − log([lactate]/[lactic acid]) = 4.80 − log(0.087/0.010) = 4.80 − log 8.7
= 4.80 − 0.94 = 3.9

2) Calculate the hydrogen concentration of a mixture of 0.10 M acetic acid and 0.20 M sodium acetate; the pKa of acetic acid is 4.76.

pH = pKa + log([acetate]/[acetic acid]) = 4.76 + log(0.20/0.10) = 4.76 + 0.30
pH = 5.1   pH = −log10[H+] ⇒ [H+] = antilog(−5.1) = 7.9 × 10−6 M
